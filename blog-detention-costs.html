What Detention Really Costs Both Sides of the Dock (TEST)

Two extra hours at your facility rarely costs two hours. Because of how driving hours work, it often costs the next delivery appointment.

Lemberg Group · 28 August 2026 · 3 min read

The clock that matters is not the one on the wall

A driver in the United States works inside three federal limits at once: up to 11 hours of actual driving, inside a 14-hour window that starts when the day starts and does not pause, and a weekly cap of 60 or 70 hours depending on the schedule. There is also a required 30-minute break after eight cumulative hours of driving.

The 14-hour window is the one that turns waiting into damage. It runs whether the truck is moving or standing at your dock. Time spent waiting does not extend it, and it cannot be recovered later in the day.

How two hours becomes a missed appointment

Take a driver who starts at six in the morning with a 500-mile run after the pickup. On a clean day the load is on the wheels by eight and the driver has plenty of window left to cover the distance legally.

Add two hours at the dock. The truck leaves at ten. The 14-hour window still ends at eight in the evening, but there are now only ten hours left in it — and inside those, mandatory break time, fuel, scale stops and traffic. The 500 miles no longer fit. The driver stops short, takes a ten-hour rest, and arrives the following morning instead of that night.

Nothing about the second delivery changed. The two hours at the first dock moved it by a day.

What it costs the carrier

A truck that is not moving is not earning, and the loss is not only the waiting time. The driver's day is consumed, the next load has to be rescheduled, and the reload the dispatcher had planned in the destination market may be gone by the time the truck arrives. Detention charges recover part of the direct cost. They do not recover the day.

What it costs the shipper

It is easy to read detention as a line item on an invoice. The real cost is quieter. Carriers keep records of which facilities load quickly and which do not, and that memory shows up in pricing and in willingness to accept the lane. A facility with a reputation for four-hour waits gets fewer offers and higher rates, and neither is usually traced back to the dock.

What actually reduces it

  • Real appointment windows. A two-hour window that is respected is worth more to a carrier than a one-hour window that never is.
  • Drop trailers where volume justifies them. If a trailer is staged at your dock, loading happens on your schedule and no clock is running. For high-volume facilities this is usually the single most effective change.
  • Tell the driver where to go. A large share of dock time is spent finding the right gate, the right office and the right person. Clear instructions on the rate confirmation cost nothing.
  • Load early in the day. A delay at eight in the morning can often be absorbed. The same delay at four in the afternoon usually cannot.

The point

Detention is not a billing dispute. It is a scheduling problem that both sides pay for, and the side that pays most is usually the one waiting at the other end for a truck that is legally unable to arrive.

If a lane keeps running into detention, tell us — the fix is usually in how the appointment is planned, not in the rate.
